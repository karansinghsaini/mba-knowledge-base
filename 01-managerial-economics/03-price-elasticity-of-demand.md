# Price Elasticity of Demand

## 3.1 Concept of Price Elasticity

* **Definition & Formula:** Price Elasticity of Demand ($PED$) is a unit-free measure that quantifies the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in its price, holding all other factors constant (*ceteris paribus*). Although price and quantity move inversely (making the raw value negative), $PED$ is typically analyzed in absolute terms:

  $$PED = \frac{\%\ \text{Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\%\ \text{Change in Price}}$$

  * **Elastic Demand ($PED > 1$):** Quantity demanded responds strongly to price changes.
  * **Inelastic Demand ($PED < 1$):** Quantity demanded responds weakly to price changes.
  * **Unitary Elastic Demand ($PED = 1$):** Percentage change in quantity demanded equals the percentage change in price.

* **Importance in Managerial Decision-Making:**
  * **Pricing Strategy:** Helps managers set optimal price points, test price sensitivity for new launches, and design price discrimination schemes (e.g., student or off-peak discounts for price-sensitive segments).
  * **Revenue Forecasting:** Predicts how price adjustments impact Total Revenue ($TR = P \times Q$), which is critical for cash-flow planning, bundling/unbundling, and dynamic pricing in industries like airlines and telecom:
    * *When Demand is Elastic ($PED > 1$):* $P \downarrow \implies Q \uparrow\uparrow \implies TR \uparrow$ (and $P \uparrow \implies TR \downarrow$).
    * *When Demand is Inelastic ($PED < 1$):* $P \uparrow \implies Q \downarrow \implies TR \uparrow$ (and $P \downarrow \implies TR \downarrow$).

## 3.2 Degrees of Price Elasticity

Consumers exhibit five distinct levels of price sensitivity, each producing a different demand curve shape:

### Visual Overview of Demand Curves

```text
Price (P)                        Price (P)                        Price (P)
  │                                │   | (D)                        │
  │                                │   |                            │ \
P ├────────────── (D)              │   |                            │   \  (Flatter)
  │                                │   |                            │     \ (D)
  └────────────── Quantity (Q)     └───┴────────── Quantity (Q)     └───────┴── Quantity (Q)
  1. Perfectly Elastic (PED = ∞)   2. Perfectly Inelastic (PED = 0) 3. Relatively Elastic (PED > 1)

Price (P)                        Price (P)
  │ \                              │ \
  │  \ (Steeper)                   │  \  (Rectangular Hyperbola)
  │   \                            │    \
  │    \ (D)                       │      \ (D)
  └─────┴──────── Quantity (Q)     └────────┴── Quantity (Q)
  4. Relatively Inelastic (PED < 1) 5. Unitary Elastic (PED = 1)
```

| Degree of Elasticity | Mathematical Value | Description & Curve Shape | Examples & Managerial Implications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Perfectly Elastic** | $PED = \infty$ | Even an infinitesimal price hike drops demand to zero; infinite quantity can be sold at the prevailing market price. **Curve:** Horizontal straight line. | **Example:** Agricultural commodities in auctions or perfectly competitive markets. Sellers are *price takers* and cannot pass cost increases onto consumers. |
| **2. Perfectly Inelastic** | $PED = 0$ | Quantity demanded remains completely unchanged regardless of price fluctuations. **Curve:** Vertical straight line. | **Example:** Life-saving drugs like insulin or emergency ambulance services. Firms hold high pricing power, though usually capped by ethics or government regulation. |
| **3. Relatively Elastic** | $PED > 1$ | A small percentage change in price causes a proportionately larger change in quantity demanded (e.g., a 5% price drop yields a 10% demand rise; $PED = 2$). **Curve:** Flatter downward slope. | **Example:** Luxury goods, branded apparel, and non-essential electronics. Raising prices sharply reduces sales volume; price cuts and discounts boost total revenue. |
| **4. Relatively Inelastic** | $0 < PED < 1$ | A large percentage change in price causes only a minor change in quantity demanded (e.g., a 20% price hike causes only a 5% demand drop; $PED = 0.25$). **Curve:** Steep downward slope. | **Example:** Basic food staples, household electricity, and daily commuting fuel. Firms can raise prices to boost revenue without losing much volume. |
| **5. Unitary Elastic** | $PED = 1$ | The percentage change in quantity demanded exactly matches the percentage change in price (e.g., a 10% price cut yields a 10% demand rise). **Curve:** Rectangular hyperbola. | **Example:** A theoretical benchmark where **Total Revenue remains constant** at every price point along the curve. |

## 3.3 Determinants of Price Elasticity

1. **Availability of Substitutes:** The closer and more numerous the substitutes, the higher the elasticity.
   * *Example:* If the price of **Pepsi** rises, consumers readily switch to **Coca-Cola** (elastic). Conversely, **table salt** and **insulin** have no close substitutes, making their demand inelastic. (Strong brand loyalty and product differentiation reduce perceived substitutability.)
2. **Nature of the Commodity (Necessity vs. Luxury):**
   * *Necessities:* Essential items like **milk**, **electricity**, and basic groceries have *inelastic* demand because households must buy them even when prices rise.
   * *Luxuries:* Discretionary items like **designer watches**, **jewelry**, and **vacations** have *elastic* demand, as purchases can be postponed or skipped during price hikes or economic downturns.
3. **Proportion of Income Spent:** Big-ticket items that consume a large share of a buyer's budget—such as **housing** or **automobiles**—exhibit *elastic* demand because price hikes heavily impact household finances. Low-cost items like **toothpaste** or **matchboxes** take up a negligible fraction of income and are *inelastic*.
4. **Time Period (Short-Run vs. Long-Run):** Demand is more *inelastic* in the short run because consumers need time to adjust habits, and becomes more *elastic* in the long run.
   * *Example:* If **petrol prices** surge today, commuters still have to fuel their cars tomorrow (inelastic). Over several years, they can switch to public transit, carpool, or buy electric/fuel-efficient vehicles (elastic).
5. **Addiction and Habits:** Habit-forming goods like **cigarettes**, **alcohol**, and **daily coffee** exhibit *inelastic* demand due to psychological or physical dependence. Governments leverage this to impose "sin taxes" that generate steady tax revenue without drastically cutting consumption.

## 3.4 Measures of Price Elasticity

### 1. Percentage (Proportional) Method
Calculates the ratio of percentage change in quantity demanded to percentage change in price using initial values as the base:

$$PED = \frac{\left(\frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{Q_1}\right)}{\left(\frac{P_2 - P_1}{P_1}\right)}$$

* **Pros & Cons:** Simple and widely used for quick estimates, but sensitive to the direction of price change (yields different values depending on whether $P_1$ or $P_2$ is used as the starting base).

### 2. Total Expenditure (Outlay) Method
Developed by Alfred Marshall, this method infers elasticity by observing how Total Expenditure ($TE = P \times Q$) changes in response to a price shift:

| Price Change | Elastic ($PED > 1$) | Inelastic ($PED < 1$) | Unitary Elastic ($PED = 1$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Price Falls ($P \downarrow$)** | $TE$ Increases ($\uparrow$) | $TE$ Decreases ($\downarrow$) | $TE$ Remains Constant ($-$) |
| **Price Rises ($P \uparrow$)** | $TE$ Decreases ($\downarrow$) | $TE$ Increases ($\uparrow$) | $TE$ Remains Constant ($-$) |

* **Example:** If price drops from $\$20$ to $\$15$ and total consumer spending rises from $\$200$ to $\$240$, demand is **elastic**.
* **Pros & Cons:** Requires only price and revenue data without complex calculations, but reveals only the *category* of elasticity rather than an exact numerical value.

### 3. Point Elasticity Method
Measures elasticity at a specific point on a demand curve for infinitesimally small price changes:

$$\text{Point } PED = \frac{dQ}{dP} \times \frac{P}{Q} \quad \text{(or } \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \times \frac{P}{Q} \text{ for linear curves)}$$

```text
Price (P)
  │ \
  │  \  Upper Segment: Elastic (PED > 1)
  │   \
  │    ●  Midpoint: Unitary Elastic (PED = 1)
  │     \
  │      \  Lower Segment: Inelastic (PED < 1)
  └───────┴──────── Quantity (Q)
```

* **Key Insight:** Along a straight-line downward-sloping demand curve, elasticity is **not constant**—it is elastic in the upper half, unitary at the midpoint, and inelastic in the lower half.

### 4. Arc Elasticity Method
Measures average elasticity over a finite range (arc) between two points on a demand curve by using the midpoint (average) of initial and final prices and quantities:

$$\text{Arc } PED = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1) / \left(\frac{Q_2 + Q_1}{2}\right)}{(P_2 - P_1) / \left(\frac{P_2 + P_1}{2}\right)}$$

* **Pros & Cons:** Eliminates directional bias and provides accurate, symmetrical estimates for substantial real-world price adjustments and marketing experiments, though it assumes linearity across the arc.
