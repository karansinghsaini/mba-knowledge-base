# Other Elasticities of Demand

## 4.1 Income Elasticity of Demand ($YED$)

* **Definition & Formula:** Income Elasticity of Demand ($YED$) measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in consumer income, holding all other factors constant (*ceteris paribus*). It helps firms forecast sales across economic cycles (booms vs. recessions) and segment markets by purchasing power:
  $$YED = \frac{\%\ \text{Change in Quantity Demanded}}{\%\ \text{Change in Consumer Income}}$$
  * **Example:** If a $10\%$ rise in consumer income increases demand for **organic foods** by $15\%$, then $YED = +1.5$ (a luxury good). Conversely, if the same $10\%$ income rise reduces demand for **instant noodles** by $5\%$, then $YED = -0.5$ (an inferior good).

### Visual Overview: Engel Curves (Income vs. Quantity Demanded)

```text
Quantity (Q)                     Quantity (Q)                     Quantity (Q)
  │         / (Luxury: YED > 1)    │ \                              │   | (Zero: YED = 0)
  │       /                        │  \                             │   |
  │     /   (Necessity: 0<YED<1)   │   \ (Inferior: YED < 0)        │   |
  │   /                            │    \                           │   |
  └──────────── Income (Y)         └─────┴────── Income (Y)         └───┴────── Income (Y)
  1. Positive YED (Normal Goods)   2. Negative YED (Inferior Goods) 3. Zero YED (Strict Necessities)
```

### Classification of Goods Based on $YED$

| Good Category | $YED$ Value | Consumer Behavior & Demand Curve Shift | Examples & Business Implications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Luxury Goods** *(Income-Elastic Normal)* | $YED > 1$ | Demand grows proportionately faster than income (e.g., $20\%$ income rise $\rightarrow$ $30\%$ holiday package demand rise; $YED = 1.5$). Demand curve shifts sharply rightward in booms. | **Examples:** Designer clothing, premium cars, fine dining, exotic vacations. Highly cyclical; thrives in economic booms but suffers sharp drops during recessions. |
| **2. Necessities** *(Income-Inelastic Normal)* | $0 < YED < 1$ | Demand increases modestly as income rises, but at a diminishing rate (e.g., $20\%$ income rise $\rightarrow$ $5\%$ bread demand rise; $YED = 0.25$). | **Examples:** Basic groceries (milk, rice, vegetables), electricity, water. Provides a stable, predictable core revenue stream across all economic conditions. |
| **3. Inferior Goods** | $YED < 0$ | Demand falls as income rises because consumers upgrade to higher-quality alternatives. Demand curve shifts inward/leftward as income grows. | **Examples:** Generic/discount food brands, second-hand clothing, public buses (replaced by personal cars/ride-sharing). Counter-cyclical: sales rise during recessions. |
| **4. Strict Necessities** *(Zero Income Elasticity)* | $YED = 0$ | Quantity demanded remains completely unaffected by income changes; governed by physiological or habitual need. | **Examples:** Table salt, basic soap/toothpaste, prescribed medications. Perfectly income-inelastic; ideal for long-term defensive investments. |

### Importance of $YED$ in Business Decisions
* **Product Targeting & Lifecycle Strategy:** Firms launch basic, value-oriented models in budget-conscious or developing regions (low/negative $YED$) and introduce premium lines or upsells in affluent, high-growth urban markets (high $YED$).
* **Market Segmentation:** Enables tiered product portfolios and pricing strategies tailored to income brackets—for instance, automakers offering **entry-level hatchbacks** for lower-income buyers, **mid-range sedans** for middle-income families, and **luxury SUVs** for high-income segments.

## 4.2 Cross Elasticity of Demand ($XED$)

* **Definition & Formula:** Cross Elasticity of Demand ($XED$) measures the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of one product (**Good X**) to a change in the price of another related product (**Good Y**), revealing interdependence in the consumer's basket:
  $$XED = \frac{\%\ \text{Change in Quantity Demanded of Good X}}{\%\ \text{Change in Price of Good Y}}$$
  * **Example:** If the price of **tea** rises by $10\%$ and the demand for **coffee** increases by $15\%$, then $XED = \frac{+15\%}{+10\%} = +1.5$, confirming that tea and coffee are close substitutes.

### Visual Overview: Cross-Price Relationships

```text
Price of Good Y (Py)             Price of Good Y (Py)             Price of Good Y (Py)
  │         /                      │ \                              │   |
  │       /                        │  \                             │   |
  │     /   (Positive: XED > 0)    │   \ (Negative: XED < 0)        │   | (Zero: XED = 0)
  │   /                            │    \                           │   |
  └──────────── Quantity of X (Qx) └─────┴────── Quantity of X (Qx) └───┴────── Quantity of X (Qx)
  1. Substitutes (Py ↑ ⟹ Qx ↑)     2. Complements (Py ↑ ⟹ Qx ↓)     3. Unrelated Goods (Py ↑ ⟹ No ΔQx)
```

### Types of Cross Elasticity

| Relationship Type | $XED$ Value | Market Mechanism | Examples & Strategic Applications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Substitutes** *(Positive $XED$)* | $XED > 0$ *(Close: $XED > 1$; Weak: $0 < XED < 1$)* | A price hike in Good Y prompts consumers to switch to Good X ($P_Y \uparrow \implies Q_X \uparrow$). Higher positive values indicate fiercer rivalry. | **Examples:** Tea & coffee, butter & margarine, Uber & local taxis. Requires close competitor price monitoring, product differentiation, and brand loyalty to avoid price wars. |
| **2. Complements** *(Negative $XED$)* | $XED < 0$ | Goods are consumed jointly; a price hike in Good Y reduces demand for Good X ($P_Y \uparrow \implies Q_X \downarrow$). Larger negative values mean stronger joint dependence. | **Examples:** Printers & ink cartridges, cars & fuel, smartphones & data plans. Supports **bundle pricing** and **cross-subsidization** (e.g., selling low-cost printers with high-margin ink). |
| **3. Unrelated Goods** *(Zero $XED$)* | $XED = 0$ | Price changes in Good Y have zero impact on the demand for Good X; the goods belong to independent consumption categories. | **Examples:** Milk & movie tickets, bread & laptops, detergent & smartphones. Pricing and promotional campaigns can be executed completely independently. |

### Managerial Uses & Applications of $XED$
1. **Pricing Strategies in Competitive Markets:** When $XED$ is high and positive (e.g., competing smartphone brands), unilateral price hikes rapidly erode market share. Firms rely on **price-matching**, **penetration pricing**, or **non-price differentiation** rather than risky price leadership.
2. **Brand Portfolio & Cannibalization Control:** Within the same company, high $XED$ between two similar products (e.g., two shampoos under one brand) risks *cannibalization*—where promoting one steals sales from the other. Managers use $XED$ to cluster offerings into distinct tiers (budget, mid-range, premium) and optimize retail shelf placement.
3. **Strategic Alliances & Partnerships:**
   * *Complementary Alliances ($XED < 0$):* Firms form co-branding or bundling partnerships to boost joint demand (e.g., **coffee machine makers partnering with coffee pod brands**, or **smartphone makers bundling telecom data plans**).
   * *Defensive Mergers ($XED > 1$):* Firms facing intense substitution may acquire or merge with close rivals to stabilize market share and reduce destructive price competition.
4. **Market Entry & Exit Decisions:** Cross elasticity helps firms decide whether or not to enter a new market based on sensitivity of demand to price changes in related goods and competitive landscape, where strong existing substitutes may deter entry. High positive $XED$ (e.g., entering a saturated soda market) warns of intense price competition and thin margins, whereas low or zero $XED$ signals an untapped niche. Conversely, a rising $XED$ caused by emerging substitutes may prompt a firm to reposition or exit a declining product line.
