# Demand Function and Law of Demand

## 2.1 Understanding Demand

* **Meaning & Definition:** Economic demand is not mere desire; it requires both the **ability to pay** (purchasing power, income, credit) and the **willingness to pay** (perceived value, urgency) at a given price and time.

* **Types of Demand:**

  * **Individual Demand:** Quantity demanded by a single consumer at various prices over a specific period (e.g., one customer buying two cups of coffee daily).

  * **Market Demand:** Total quantity demanded by all consumers in the market—the horizontal sum of all individual demand schedules (e.g., total laptop demand in a city).

  * **Joint Demand:** Two or more complementary goods used together to satisfy a single need (e.g., printers and ink cartridges, cars and fuel).

  * **Composite Demand:** A single commodity demanded for multiple distinct uses (e.g., milk used for drinking, cheese, and curd; steel used in construction and vehicles).

## 2.2 Demand Function

* **Definition & Purpose:** A mathematical tool expressing the relationship between quantity demanded ($Q_d$) and its influencing factors. It helps managers set optimal prices, plan production, forecast revenue, and evaluate policy impacts.

* **Functional Forms:**

  * *Simple Linear Form:* $Q_d = a - bP$ (isolates the price effect, where $a$ and $b$ are constants).

  * *Multivariate Form:* $Q_d = f(P, I, P_r, T, E, N)$ (e.g., $Q_d = 120 - 5P + 0.2I - 3P_r + 4T + 2E + 10N$), used in regression and scenario analysis. Here, I is Income, Pr is price of related goods, T is tastes and preferences, E is exceptions and N is number of buyers.

  *Importance in Business Decision-Making:* It helps in Price setting, production planning, marketing strategy, budgeting & forecasting and policy formulation.

* **Determinants of Demand:**

  1. **Price of the Good ($P$):** The most direct determinant; exhibits an inverse relationship with quantity demanded (*ceteris paribus*).

  2. **Income of Consumers ($I$):** Demand rises with income for **normal goods** (and luxury goods with high income elasticity) but falls for **inferior goods** (e.g., low-cost staples replaced by premium options).

  3. **Prices of Related Goods ($P_r$):**

     * *Substitutes:* Price increase in one raises demand for the other (e.g., tea and coffee).

     * *Complements:* Price increase in one lowers demand for the other (e.g., smartphones and data plans).

  4. **Tastes & Preferences ($T$):** Shaped by cultural trends, advertising, and innovation; shifts the entire demand curve.

  5. **Expectations ($E$):** Anticipated future price hikes or income growth boost current demand, whereas expected recessions delay spending.

  6. **Number of Buyers ($N$):** Population growth and market expansion increase aggregate market demand.

## 2.3 Law of Demand

* **Statement:** Other things being equal (*ceteris paribus*), the quantity demanded of a good falls when its price rises ($P \uparrow \implies Q_d \downarrow$) and rises when its price falls ($P \downarrow \implies Q_d \uparrow$).

* **Demand Schedule & Curve:** A demand schedule tabulates quantities demanded at different prices. Plotting price (Y-axis) against quantity demanded (X-axis) yields a **downward-sloping demand curve**.

* **Key Assumptions (*Ceteris Paribus*):** Non-price determinants — consumer income, tastes, prices of related goods, population size, and future expectations — must remain constant to isolate the pure price effect.

* **Reasons for the Downward Slope:**

  1. **Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility:** Successive units yield less additional satisfaction, so consumers only buy more at lower prices.

  2. **Substitution Effect:** A price drop makes the good relatively cheaper than substitutes, prompting consumers to switch.

  3. **Income Effect:** A lower price increases consumers' real purchasing power, allowing them to buy more.

  4. **Entry of New Buyers:** Lower prices make the product affordable to lower-income segments, expanding the buyer base.

  5. **Multiple Uses:** When prices fall, consumers allocate multi-use commodities (e.g., electricity) to secondary, less essential uses.

## 2.4 Exceptions to the Law of Demand

In certain scenarios, demand curves slope upward or remain unresponsive to price changes:

| Exception Type | Core Mechanism | Real-World Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Giffen Goods** | Rare inferior staples taking up a large share of income; a price hike reduces real income so severely that consumers cut costlier foods and buy *more* of the staple (negative income effect outweighs substitution effect). | Bread, rice, or coarse grains in low-income households. |
| **Veblen Goods** | Driven by *conspicuous consumption* and prestige; higher prices signal exclusivity and status, increasing appeal. | Luxury cars, designer handbags, high-end watches, rare art. |
| **Speculative Situations** | Driven by investment expectations rather than utility; rising prices trigger expectations of further appreciation, prompting immediate buying. | Real estate, stocks, cryptocurrencies, gold, and oil. |
| **Necessities & Perception** | Essential goods with **price-inelastic demand**; consumers must purchase roughly the same quantity regardless of short-term price hikes. | Insulin, petrol for daily commuting, household electricity. |
